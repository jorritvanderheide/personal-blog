A blog is a stream. New posts push old posts down. The most recent thing is always the most visible. A digital garden is the opposite: pages grow and change over time, and the most developed ones are the most visible.

<!--more-->

### The stream problem

Reverse chronological order rewards frequency over depth. A blog post published today is more prominent than a carefully revised essay from last year, regardless of which is more useful. The format incentivizes publishing something new rather than improving something old.

This is fine for news. It is bad for knowledge. Most of what I know did not arrive in neat, date-stamped installments. It accumulated slowly, was revised, was sometimes wrong and later corrected.

### What a garden does differently

In a digital garden, pages have a status: seedling, growing, evergreen. A seedling is a rough idea, barely formed. A growing page has some substance but is not finished. An evergreen page is mature and reliable.

Readers can see the state of each page. They know that a seedling might be wrong. They know that an evergreen page has been tended. This honesty about completeness is rare on the web.

### Why not both

This site is a log, not a garden — for now. But the library section is garden-like: a collection that grows and is curated over time. The distinction is less about technology and more about intention. A log records. A garden cultivates.
